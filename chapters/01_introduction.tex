\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\paragraph{Motivation}
In the production systems described as Industry~4.0, machines, sensors and software are
connected, and many decisions once made by a worker are now made on the basis of data
recorded during production~\cite{lasi2014industry}. Automated visual inspection uses
cameras to check whether a part is free of defects or to identify its material,
e.g., before it is sorted or processed further~\cite{czimmermann2020visual, penumuru2020material}. For such image
tasks, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become a standard tool, because they
learn the relevant features directly from labelled images instead of relying on rules
designed by hand~\cite{lecun2015deep}.

A camera in a production cell, however, rarely sees the workpiece alone. Around it lie
conveyor belts, grippers, cables and other machine parts, and some of them share the
colour or the surface of the workpiece. A CNN trained on such images is not told which
pixels belong to the workpiece. It may therefore draw on the surroundings as well as on
the workpiece, and its accuracy in the setting it was trained in does not reveal this. A model that has learned a background cue may fail
as soon as the background changes~\cite{xiao2021noise}, e.g., when the workpiece appears
at another station. Accuracy alone therefore cannot show whether a decision rests on the
workpiece or on the background. A heatmap can indicate it, but a heatmap is an
approximation that may miss evidence the model uses or show importance where the model
uses none~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}.

Studying this question in a running factory is difficult, since its scenes cannot be
repeated at will. Learning factories, i.e., scaled-down production environments built
for teaching and research~\cite{kemeny2016complementary}, offer a controlled
alternative. They contain scaled models of the machines of a production line and a simpler version
of its visual complexity. In the learning factory of this thesis, the workpieces, their
positions and the lighting were chosen and recorded
systematically~\cite{schneidereitYOLOv8}.

\paragraph{The learning factory and its dataset}
This thesis works with the Fischertechnik Industry~4.0 learning factory, a model
production line with five modules, namely a sorting machine, an oven, a milling machine,
a high-bay warehouse and a vacuum gripper~\cite{schneidereitFischertechnik2023,
schneidereitYOLOv8}. Its red and blue building blocks, its many black parts, its cables
and its grey base plate form a complex background for any workpiece placed in it. An
earlier study by S.~Schneidereit et al.~\cite{schneidereitFischertechnik2023},
presented at IntelliSys 2023 and published in 2024 and referred to below as the 2023
study, applied
YOLO (You Only Look Once) object detectors~\cite{redmon2016yolo} to the learning factory,
with a dataset of its own, and already linked detection errors to workpieces that share
their colour with the background.

The dataset of this thesis was created in the later study by T.~Schneidereit, Gohrenz
and Breu{\ss}~\cite{schneidereitYOLOv8}, referred to below as the 2025 study. It
contains 15 cylindrical workpieces of different materials and colours, one physical piece
each, namely six plastics, four workpieces of acrylic glass (AG), three metals and two
woods; the wooden ones are simple dowels (Section~\ref{sec:background-dataset}). Each
workpiece was photographed at the same 33 positions in each of the five stations of the
learning factory (34 at station five in the copy used here,
Section~\ref{sec:background-dataset}). Lighting and camera position changed between positions, but at a given
position they are the same for all 15 workpieces, so that at one position only the
workpiece changes. Several workpieces were chosen on purpose because they resemble
parts of the learning factory, e.g., the black workpiece because it matches the many
black factory parts. With YOLOv8 detectors and heatmaps computed by
layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP)~\cite{bach2015lrp, montavon2019lrp}, the 2025
study found that some background parts influenced the detection, and black plastic was the
workpiece that its YOLOv8n models detected least accurately. Section~\ref{sec:related-work-fischertechnik} reviews both
studies, and Section~\ref{sec:background-dataset} describes the copy of the dataset used
here.

\paragraph{Why the object-to-image ratio matters}
Both studies examined detection, i.e., locating the workpiece with a bounding box in the
whole photograph. In the $960 \times 720$-pixel photographs of the 2025 study, the median
box covers about 0.33\% of the image, and nearly everything else shows the learning
factory. A classifier that decides on the material of a workpiece can instead work on a
crop around it, and the tightness of that crop sets how much of the input is workpiece
and how much is background. This share is the object-to-image (O2I) ratio, the area of
the object divided by that of the image~\cite{pawlowski2019needles}. They treat the ratio as an experimental variable for
the classification of small objects in large images, e.g., lesions in histopathology
images, and find that the task becomes harder as the ratio falls. For a fixed amount of
training data, there is a ratio below which none of their models performs better than
chance. The ratios studied here, from 90\% down to 10\%, lie well above that limit in their
experiments, but with far less training data here (at most 116 positive photographs per
model), the limit is likely higher.

A lower ratio does more than make the object small relative to the image. It also gives
the model more background from which to draw evidence. Xiao et
al.~\cite{xiao2021noise} showed for ImageNet classifiers that the background alone can
carry enough information for a non-trivial accuracy, and that, with adversarially chosen
backgrounds, models can be made to predict the class of the background for up to 87.5\%
of the foregrounds. Background
evidence can thus outweigh the object. In the learning factory, whose workpieces were
several were chosen to resemble the background, this raises a concrete question. As the crop
around a workpiece is loosened and the learning factory takes up more of it, at which
point does the background begin to dominate the decision instead of the workpiece? The
classification score alone cannot answer it. It can show that the classification gets
worse as the ratio falls, but not whether the background is the reason.

\paragraph{Why explainability, and why two methods}
Attribution methods for CNNs answer a different question from accuracy, namely which
regions of one input a method credits with the decision. Their output is a heatmap over
the image. Occlusion sensitivity, introduced by Zeiler and
Fergus~\cite{zeilerFergus2014}, covers one region of the input at a time with a grey
patch and records how much the score of the class drops, so that a large drop marks a
region to whose replacement the score is sensitive. It is the method specified for this thesis. Gradient-weighted
Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), introduced by Selvaraju et
al.~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, takes a different route. It leaves the input unchanged
and uses the gradients of the class score with respect to the feature maps of a
convolutional layer to weight those maps, which it combines into one coarse map in a
single backward pass. Grad-CAM was added because it belongs to a different family of
methods: occlusion sensitivity changes the input, Grad-CAM reads gradients inside the
network.

A second method is needed because a heatmap is not ground truth for what the network
uses~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}, and different methods can give different answers for
the same decision (Section~\ref{sec:related-work-explainability}). A shift of the
evidence that only one method shows, or that one method misses, could then be a property
of that method rather than of the model. This is why both methods are applied to the
same crops, why each of their results is also read against the value that a map spread uniformly
over the bounding box would give at that method's resolution, and why both are finally checked against direct tests that remove
the workpiece or the background from the crop and measure how the decision changes.

To compare heatmaps across ratios, this thesis reduces each map to a single number that
says whether its importance lies on the workpiece or on the background. It introduces
the centre measure $c$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:center-frac}; Fig.~\ref{fig:bg-centre-toy} shows
it on a toy map), a per-pixel variant of the energy-based pointing
game~\cite{wang2020scorecam}. It compares the mean heatmap importance
per pixel inside a centred reference square, whose area is $r\%$ of the crop at an O2I
ratio of $r\%$, with the mean importance per pixel outside it. A flat map gives $c =
0.5$ at every ratio. A value above 0.5 means that a pixel of the square carries more
importance on average than a pixel outside it, and a value below 0.5 the opposite. The
background is taken to dominate only where $c$ lies clearly below 0.5, i.e., where a
test over the 15 materials places it below 0.5 with $p < 0.01$. The same measure,
computed from the drop of the class score when the square or its outside is removed as a
whole, gives an intervention centre measure that does not depend on any heatmap. A trend
in the centre measure of a method is read as a property of the models only if this
direct test shows it too (Section~\ref{sec:methodology-rq}).

\paragraph{Research question}
The thesis addresses the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{At what object-to-image ratio does the complex factory background begin to
dominate the model's classification decision, and can explainability methods detect
this shift?}
\end{quote}
The first part asks whether, and from which ratio on, the background outweighs the
workpiece in the evidence behind the decision. The second asks whether occlusion
sensitivity and Grad-CAM register such a shift, and whether what they register is a
property of the models.

\paragraph{Problem statement and contribution}
The aim of this thesis is to measure how the share of complex background in the input
affects the material classification of a CNN, whether the decision moves from the
workpiece to the background, and whether two explainability methods detect such a shift
correctly. The
reviewed literature covers the parts of this question separately
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-gap}). Pawlowski et al.\ vary the O2I ratio but use a
single saliency method, as a localisation check. The two studies on the learning factory
detect workpieces against its complex background, and only the later one computes
heatmaps, with one method. The studies of background reliance reviewed here change the
background or the context of an image rather than the share of the image that the object
covers. The
contribution of this thesis is a comparison of two explainability methods on the same
crops as a function of the O2I ratio, against a complex industrial background, with a
per-pixel measure of whether the evidence concentrates on the workpiece or on the
background, checked against direct tests that remove the workpiece or the background.

For this comparison, crops centred on the workpiece were cut from the photographs of the
dataset at five ratios, 90\%, 70\%, 50\%, 30\% and 10\%. Since all crops are resized to
the same input size, the share of background and the scale of the workpiece change
together, so the effect of the ratio found here is their combined effect
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-limitations}). For each of the 15 materials and each ratio,
a binary one-vs-rest CNN that separates one material from the other 14 was trained from
scratch, without a pre-trained model, which gives 75 models trained with the same
configuration and seed, hence from the same initial weights (Sections~\ref{sec:methodology-ovr}
and~\ref{sec:methodology-training}). Classification is
measured with the F1 score of the target material on a held-out test split. Occlusion
sensitivity and Grad-CAM were applied to the same 10 validation crops of each model,
drawn separately for each model from its target material, i.e., 750 analyses per
method. Besides the level 0.5, each centre measure is read
against a reference value, introduced during the analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology-reference}), the value that a map lying uniformly on the bounding box of
the workpiece would give at the resolution of each method. Five robustness checks test
how far the results depend on the occlusion normalisation, the fill of the occluding
patch, the interpolation of the maps, the flat Grad-CAM maps and the centring of the
crops, and an extension to 200 epochs tests the training length. Finally, the workpiece
or the background is removed from the crops to test the heatmaps directly, Grad-CAM is
recomputed in three variants, among them HiResCAM~\cite{draelos2021hirescam}, which
avoids its averaging of gradients, and the untrained network serves as a control.

The results answer the two parts of the question as follows. As the ratio falls from
90\% to 10\%, the mean test F1 over the 15 materials drops from 0.813 to 0.720, and most
of this loss occurs below 50\%. The background, however, does not take over the
decision at any of these ratios. With the workpiece filled in, the models can no longer
tell the materials apart (mean ROC-AUC 0.44 to 0.50); with the background filled in,
they rank the crops as well as before (0.98 at every ratio); and the intervention centre
measure lies well above 0.5 at every ratio without falling. The errors are mostly
confusions between workpieces of similar colour, above all among aluminium, steel and
grey plastic.

For the second part, the two methods disagree. The Grad-CAM centre measure falls from
0.737 to 0.407, against a reference value that rises as the ratio falls, so neither the
geometry of the square nor the resolution of the method produces the fall. The occlusion
centre measure rises from 0.728 to 0.884. The direct tests side with occlusion
sensitivity, the method specified for this thesis. The fall of Grad-CAM comes from the
method itself: it averages gradients over a map whose positions the network weights
differently, and as the workpiece shrinks, the average is formed more and more over the
background. HiResCAM, which uses the same gradients without averaging them, rises like
occlusion. In this network and over these ratios, Grad-CAM therefore reports a shift of
the evidence that the models do not show.

The materials also differ beyond the effect of the ratio. Steel and aluminium are the
weakest at every ratio, already at 90\%, and fail mainly on look-alike workpieces rather
than on the background. Black plastic, which the 2025 study detected worst, has the
highest mean test F1 here (0.956); the two studies differ in task, metric and training.

\paragraph{Structure of the thesis}
Chapter~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews the earlier work on the learning factory and the
literature on the O2I ratio, background reliance, explainability and small objects, and
states the gap this thesis fills. Chapter~\ref{sec:background} introduces the dataset, the O2I ratio,
the CNN and its training, the two explainability methods and the comparison of object
and background in a heatmap, on a toy example and on one real crop.
Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the crops and their split, the one-vs-rest
design, the training, the explainability pipeline, the reduction of a heatmap to the
centre measure and how the results are aggregated and read.
Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} also defines the direct tests of the heatmaps.
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} reports the classification results, the centre measures of
both methods with their reference values, the direct tests, the robustness checks and
the 200-epoch extension. Finally, Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} answers the research question,
discusses the materials that depart from the average and closes with the limitations,
future work and a conclusion.
